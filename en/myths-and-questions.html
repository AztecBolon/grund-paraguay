Myths About Relocating to Paraguay

We break down five common claims about relocating to Paraguay — from "citizenship in a year" to myths about visas and crypto transfers — with a source cited for each point.

In the market for relocation services to Paraguay, a set of claims keeps coming up often enough to be worth examining separately, with a source noted for each one.

“Citizenship in one year”

Not true. Paraguay's Constitution (Art. 148) requires a minimum of three years of "radicación" — and this period is counted from the date permanent residency is granted, not from the moment of entering the country. The official page of Paraguay's judiciary (Poder Judicial) confirms this. Once you add the time needed to obtain residency itself, the real overall timeline is usually well beyond a year — more on this on the "Why Paraguay" page.

“Residency through a $5,000 deposit”

This scheme really did exist, but it was abolished back in October 2022 along with the new migration law (Ley 6984/22). Instead of a fixed deposit, a system now applies that's tied to specific proof of solvency or to the applicant's investment project, rather than to one universal amount.

“Russians need a visa to apply for residency”

Not true regarding entry — Russia and Paraguay have had a visa-free regime since 2014 (more on the "Visa and Entry" page). A separate procedural nuance — whether you can formally file residency paperwork while already in Paraguay on visa-free tourist status — isn't confirmed by a direct official DNM statement.

“Paraguay is on the FATF black or grey list, so banking is especially risky”

Not true: as of mid-2026, the country isn't on that list (more on the "Banking and Money Transfers" page).

“Crypto and stablecoins are a risk-free way to move money around restrictions”

Not true: since fiscal year 2026, mandatory tax reporting on crypto assets has applied in Paraguay, and the country's Central Bank officially labels cryptocurrencies as high-risk assets with no status as legal tender.

We couldn't find a documented official warning from Paraguay's migration authority specifically about fraudulent agents and intermediaries (along the lines of similar warnings published by the migration authorities of Chile and Peru) — that doesn't mean such a warning doesn't exist; it may simply not be available in open sources. General caution when choosing an agency in this market is discussed in more detail on the "Honestly, About the Risks" page.
