Healthcare in Paraguay

How healthcare works in Paraguay for a foreigner: the system's three levels, what private insurance costs, and what to check in the contract before signing it.

There's almost no Russian-language content about healthcare in Paraguay, and what exists is either tourist gloss or advertising for a specific insurer. Here's a practical breakdown: what you get for free, what you'll have to pay for, and which contract terms with an insurer are easy to miss if you're not reading it in your native language.

Three levels of the system

Paraguay runs three levels of healthcare at once, and it's easy to confuse them. MSPBS (the Ministry of Public Health) runs public hospitals and clinics — basic care, widely available, with residency status not a condition for walking through the door. IPS (Instituto de Previsión Social) isn't public healthcare in general — it's a separate social insurance system tied to formal employment: it provides comprehensive free care (basic consultations, complex surgeries, treatment after traffic accidents, treatment of existing conditions), but access comes through payroll contributions, not simply from being in the country. The third level is private medicine — clinics and insurance plans that most foreigners end up using regardless of status. The difference between MSPBS and IPS is the single most common point of confusion in English-language sources, and it's exactly what explains why different sites answer "is healthcare free for a foreigner?" so differently.

IPS: who has access and what it covers

The IPS contribution is 25.5% of salary, split 9% employee / 16.5% employer. Registration requires a Paraguayan cédula, proof of employment, and the employer's registration in the social security system — meaning access comes together with formal employment, not automatically from residency status. Temporary residents don't have automatic access to IPS: they can use MSPBS for basic care, but reliable access to specialists usually requires private insurance or a prepaid plan. Separately: a freelancer can voluntarily join the pension side of IPS, but not the medical side — voluntary enrollment specifically in medical coverage isn't available to the self-employed; registering as a microentrepreneur for this will only become mandatory starting in 2028, meaning as of 2026 this isn't a rule in effect yet, just a future one.

Sources describe tourists' access to public hospitals differently — anywhere from "emergency care only" to "completely free for anyone with an ID." We couldn't find a direct, unambiguous answer specifically for tourist status — if this matters for your situation, we confirm it separately rather than relying on a general statement.

Private medicine: local and international plans

The Spanish-language market uses the term medicina prepaga (prepaid medical plan) for an HMO-style policy — a category distinct from "seguro médico" (a classic reimbursement-type policy). Local insurers have quirks worth knowing in advance. For example, MAPFRE Paraguay (the "Trébol Salud Global" plan) offers two options — 80% or 100% reimbursement (including maternity, up to $7,000) — but requires permanent-resident status: at the temporary-residency stage, that plan simply isn't available. Another major local player, Asismed, has strict waiting periods ("carencias"): 6 months for declared chronic conditions, at least 12 months for cancer and kidney/liver disease, and undeclared conditions aren't covered at all; a health declaration is mandatory when signing up, and claim documents must be submitted within 48 hours. Insurers don't publicly disclose pricing for local prepaid plans, but three independent sources converge on a rough benchmark of about $70–150/month for a mid-to-premium plan (individual sources cite a wider range — $25 to $300 — but those figures don't overlap across sources). International insurance (Cigna Global, Allianz Care, April International, and others) costs more — roughly $150–800/month depending on coverage — but in exchange gives access to a broader clinic network and doesn't require residency. If you're choosing an international plan, it's worth checking six things: translation of medical reports into a language you understand, evacuation coverage, worldwide vs. region-only coverage, terms on pre-existing conditions, the deductible structure, and coverage for emergency care and hospitalization.

Clinics

The UK's official traveler list names, with contact details, two private facilities in Asunción with English-speaking staff: Centro Médico Bautista (Avda. República Argentina 635 y Campos Cervera, +595 21 6889000) and Centro Médico La Costa (Av. Artigas 1500 y María Concepción Leyes de Chávez, +595 21 2171000) — with the caveat that the list isn't exhaustive and isn't a recommendation of any specific clinic. Asunción is also home to the private hospital Sanatorio Británico (Av. Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia 910) — its range of services and its work with insurance networks weren't separately verified.

Risks that make insurance more than a formality

Dengue is a real seasonal risk, not a rarity; details and sources are on the "Myths & Questions" page, "Health & Climate" section. Separately, the UK's official travel portal notes that private clinics in Paraguay usually require payment on the spot, without waiting for reimbursement from the insurer — meaning insurance isn't "just in case," it's a working tool for year one.
