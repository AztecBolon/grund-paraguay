Why Paraguay, and Not Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, or Brazil

An honest comparison of relocation conditions: why the short citizenship timelines advertised in neighboring countries almost never apply to a typical person relocating — and what sets Paraguay apart against that backdrop.

If you compare the countries of the region using a single figure — "how many years until citizenship" — the choice doesn't look like it favors Paraguay at first glance:

CountryStandard time to citizenshipCondition for a shorter time
Argentina2 years— this is the standard time, no extra conditions
Chile5 years2 years — only when married to a Chilean citizen
Brazil4 years1 year — only for citizens of Portuguese-speaking countries or when married to a Brazilian citizen
Uruguay3 years (with a "family established" in the country) / 5 yearsa separate tax residency for investors — from ≈$377,000
Paraguay7–8 years (realistic timeline)the path is open without marrying a local citizen, without third-country citizenship, and without a deposit of hundreds of thousands of dollars

On paper, Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, and Brazil look faster. In practice, three of the four short timelines come with a condition that rules out most people who relocate.

For Chile and Brazil, the short timeline isn't the general path — it's the exception. Chile's two years is a reduction only "for qualifying ties," which in practice means marriage to a citizen of the country; the standard path takes 5 years. Brazil's one year applies only to citizens of Portuguese-speaking countries or those married to a Brazilian citizen; without that, it's 4 years — not meaningfully faster than Paraguay at all. If the person relocating has no local spouse and no citizenship of Portugal, Angola, or Mozambique, these figures simply don't apply to them.

Uruguay's situation is different: three years with a "family established" in the country (five without one) sound appealing on their own, but the "fast Uruguay" version that usually shows up in advertising is a separate tax residency for investors, starting at roughly $377,000 (the threshold has been in place since the 2020 reform). By comparison, the investor track in Paraguay (more in our services) opens at $70,000. For someone with income from remote work rather than substantial capital, the Uruguayan option isn't "a pricier alternative" — it's effectively out of reach.

Argentina is the strongest competitor in this table, and there's nothing to debunk about the substance of its conditions: two years of continuous residence is one of the shortest timelines in the world, with no hidden catches. But that formal advantage has a flip side: since Javier Milei came to power in late 2023, Argentina's migration policy has been tightening steadily. Decree No. 366/2025, in force since May 29, 2025, introduced a requirement of two years of continuous residence without leaving the country for citizenship — replacing the previous practice of accumulating time through temporary visa renewals — shortened the validity of temporary status ("precaria") from 180 to 90 days, restricted free healthcare and higher education to permanent residents only, and expanded the grounds for deportation. Another decree (No. 681/2026), in force since July 31, 2026, allows denying entry to and expelling foreigners for public statements deemed hostile toward Argentina; at the time of checking, the decree was still going through mandatory review by a parliamentary committee. The statistics confirm the tightening in practice: border entry refusals show a sharp jump over the last two months — in December 2025–January 2026 alone, authorities reported almost 5,000 refusals and expulsions, noticeably above the usual monthly pace. Deportations of convicted foreigners rose by half. If the deciding factor in choosing a country is the speed of citizenship on paper by itself, the honest answer on that specific metric is Argentina, not Paraguay — but that same paper is becoming less predictable in practice, year by year.

Here's what a person choosing Paraguay actually gets. First, accessibility without workarounds: the standard path isn't tied to marrying a local citizen, holding another country's citizenship, or having substantial capital, and the faster investor track is already open from $70,000. Second, an already noticeable and growing Russian-speaking presence: in 2025, 509 Russian citizens received residency in Paraguay, and in the first quarter of 2026 alone, already 194 — almost as many as in the entire previous year. Third, the market for relocation-related services here isn't saturated yet: for the business purchase and sale market, for instance, the major regional deal trackers don't even include Paraguay in their coverage. That can be read not as a sign of underdevelopment, but as meaning fewer intermediaries competing for clients and fewer already-entrenched, rigid practices.

This accessibility comes at a cost — time. The realistic timeline "from arrival to passport" is 7 to 8 years, not one year, as some unscrupulous agencies sometimes promise; that's the direct assessment of the director of Paraguay's migration authority (DNM), Jorge Kronawetter (ABC Color, 05/04/2026) — the same figure already mentioned on the Home page. Even after meeting the formal three-year requirement following permanent residency, the decision on citizenship isn't made by the migration authority but by the Supreme Court (Corte Suprema de Justicia) — with mandatory personal appearance and legal representation.
